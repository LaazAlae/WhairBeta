Competitor Overview
Below is a breakdown of each competitor based on research across their websites, app listings,
press coverage, investor announcements, job postings, vendor case studies, and available user
feedback.

Loti AI
Founded by Luke Arrigoni, Rebekah Arrigoni, and Hirak Chhatbar. Arrigoni previously ran an AI
consulting firm and had a data science role at CAA. The company has raised $22.85M total ($6.65M
seed from FUSE, Bling Capital, Khosla Ventures, Ensemble, Alpha Edison, K5 Tokyo Black + $16.2M
Series A led by Khosla Ventures).
What they actually do: Loti is an internet-scale face and voice scanning platform. Users enroll via a
live video selfie (liveness check, not just a photo upload), and Loti scans the public internet daily
including social media and adult sites. Users review detections and choose to authorize or remove
content. Loti claims a 95% takedown success rate within 17 hours and has partnerships with WME
and CAA for talent protection and CMG Worldwide for estate/legacy protection.
Under the hood: A Weaviate case study confirms Loti processes 120-140 million images daily,
manages approximately 9 billion facial vectors, uses 3.5 PB on AWS S3, and runs extensive GPU
infrastructure. Their co-founder confirmed 40+ proprietary ML models for facial and voice recognition.
Their job postings reveal a stack of Python/Node.js, PostgreSQL/MongoDB/DynamoDB,
Docker/Kubernetes, PyTorch/TensorFlow, and Weaviate as the vector database. A Web3 DevOps
posting references IPFS, Arweave, and blockchain nodes, suggesting exploratory on-chain
enforcement/registry work.
Platform partnerships: The Weaviate case study mentions TikTok, Meta, and Pinterest as platforms
where Loti has relationships to expedite takedowns.
Gaps and weaknesses: Consumer app has almost no public reviews on iOS or Google Play,
meaning real user validation of UX and false positive rates is thin. Loti itself frames the DMCA as
limited for likeness harms, relying on partnerships to close gaps. Their API docs appear
gated/access-controlled, not publicly available. Voice protection is marketed but the actual
mechanism (watermarking vs fingerprinting vs deepfake detection) is not documented. No C2PA or
content provenance features.

Helos AI
Founded by Adi Kol and Keano Katalan, based in Deerfield Beach, FL. Listed as 2-10 employees
on LinkedIn. No public funding rounds found.

What they claim to do: Helos markets itself as a real-time impersonation defense platform for
agents, managers, IP attorneys, and brand teams. They claim active monitoring, instant takedown
protocols, evidence bundles (content hashes, timestamps, screenshots, historical change logs), and
automatic licensing and royalty collection. Their LinkedIn adds claims of cryptographic registration of
Natural Rights and licensing via smart contracts.
What is actually verifiable: Very little beyond marketing copy. The entire product is demo-gated
with no public signup, no app store presence, no self-serve funnel, and no credible third-party demo
walkthroughs. No pricing is posted. No independent validation of any technical claims was found.
The scope of claims (global monitoring, evidence-grade logging, takedown automation, smart
contract licensing, royalty automation) versus the team size of 2-10 people raises questions about
execution capacity.
Confidence assessment: Their monitoring and enforcement workflow is likely real at some level.
Evidence collection claims are unverified. Smart contract licensing and automatic royalty collection
are marketing only based on available evidence.

AXM
Founded by Archie Davis (CEO), Andrew Farrior (COO), and Jameson Parker (CTO). Listed as
2-10 employees. No public funding rounds found.
What they do: AXM is building what they call Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) for cultural IP. They
focus on registering, governing, and licensing cultural assets using stable identifiers (AXM IDs),
consent registries, and fallback licensing mechanisms. They model their approach after national
identity DPI systems like Aadhaar. Axios confirmed a pilot involving Malcolm X’s estate and Katt
Williams’ company. AXM claims to have indexed over $50M in cultural assets on their beta platform.
Programs and credibility signals: Accepted into NVIDIA Inception (a free startup program offering
co-marketing and resources, not investment). Claims selection for WIPO’s Scale Up Your IP program
and membership in the Creative Commons Open Culture Platform. Claims UNIDO digital innovation
award, though the captured source only shows AXM as a shortlisted finalist, not a confirmed winner.
Critical gap: AXM has no detection capability at all. They are a rights registry and governance layer,
not a scanning or enforcement product. They could be complementary to a detection platform but are
not competitive with one. They are also not shipping a consumer product, everything is pilot-based
and institutional.

Where We Stand

Where We Are Ahead
• Verification is confirmed white space. None of the three competitors are leading with
C2PA, content provenance, or proof-of-origin as a core product feature. Loti does a live
identity check at onboarding but that is user verification, not content verification. Helos
collects evidence artifacts but does not use any standardized provenance framework. AXM is
the closest philosophically with their authorship registry but they do not mention C2PA and
are not shipping a consumer-facing product.
• YouTube is already adopting C2PA. YouTube has rolled out content labels based on the
C2PA standard, and only devices and tools supporting C2PA 2.1+ can generate the metadata
for those labels. This means the ecosystem is moving toward exactly what our Verification
pillar is built around.
• The integrated four-pillar loop is unique. Loti and Helos are detection and enforcement
focused. AXM is verification and monetization focused. No one is connecting all four into a
single closed-loop system. Detection alone is a commodity. The loop is the moat.
• Compliance framing is timely. The regulatory landscape is accelerating (covered in detail
later in this document) and positions Whair as infrastructure that platforms and brands will
need, not just a creator tool.

Where We Are Behind
• Loti has massive operational scale. 120-140 million images processed daily, 9 billion facial
vectors, 3.5 petabytes on S3, 40+ proprietary ML models, and $22.85M in funding. We cannot
out-scale them at beta. We should not try.
• Platform relationships for faster takedowns. Loti has confirmed relationships with TikTok,
Meta, and Pinterest that expedite removals. They also have partnerships with WME, CAA,
and CMG Worldwide that give them access to high-profile talent as early customers. These
relationships take time to build.
• Consumer onboarding polish. Loti has mobile apps on iOS and Google Play with a live
video selfie enrollment flow. A beta user comparing us to Loti will expect at minimum a
smooth identity enrollment, a dashboard consolidating detections and actions, and clear
enforcement workflows.
• Voice is the weakest area industry-wide. Even Loti, which markets voice recognition and
protection and collects audio clips during onboarding, does not publicly document what their
voice protection actually does technically. There is no equivalent of PimEyes for voice.
Detecting an AI-generated voice clone (for example someone using a creator’s voice to make
a song) requires speaker verification plus deepfake audio classification, and the tooling for
this is still early across the board.

Features We Need to Account For in Beta
Based on competitor products, these are the table-stakes features a beta user will expect from a
product in this space:

• Identity enrollment that goes beyond “upload a selfie.” Loti’s live video capture sets the
expectation. At minimum we need multi-photo capture with some form of liveness or manual
verification.
• A monitoring and alerting loop. Daily scans or near-real-time alerts when something is
found.
• Case management. A list of incidents with URLs, timestamps, screenshots, match
confidence, and status tracking to support takedowns and disputes.
• Takedown initiation. Templated notices and workflows that route to the right platform
reporting forms.

Voice: The Industry-Wide Gap
Voice protection is the weakest area across all competitors and across the available tooling
landscape. This deserves specific attention because it directly impacts how we scope the beta.

What Exists Today
• Speaker verification / voice matching: Tools like Resemblyzer (open source) and Pindrop
can compare a voice sample against a reference voiceprint and determine if it is the same
speaker. This is useful for detecting if someone is using a creator’s actual voice recordings.
• AI voice / deepfake detection: Resemble AI offers a deepfake audio detector. Eleven Labs
has a voice detection classifier. These can classify whether audio is AI-generated versus real.
This matters for the scenario where someone clones a creator’s voice to make new content
(like an AI-generated song using a real artist’s voice).

What Does Not Exist
• There is no PimEyes equivalent for voice. There is no tool that crawls the internet and
finds every instance of a specific voice. Face search has mature API options; voice search at
internet scale does not.
• Even Loti’s voice protection is opaque. They collect audio clips during onboarding and
market voice recognition, but the actual technical mechanism is not documented anywhere
publicly.

Recommendation for Beta
Focus the beta on face and image detection where the APIs are mature and proven. Voice should be
scoped as a registration and verification feature (creator registers their voiceprint as a
proof-of-ownership record) rather than an active scanning feature. Full voice detection can be
phased in as the tooling matures.

Detection Tools Landscape
Beyond the APIs listed in the sprint brief, here is additional context on the broader detection tooling
available:

Reverse Image / Face Search
• TinEye API: Commercial reverse image search with published pricing. Starter at $200/5,000
searches, Basic at $300/10,000, Corporate at $1,000/50,000. Good for finding where a
specific image appears online (near-duplicate matching), but it is not a face-embedding
engine. It matches the image itself, not the person’s face across different photos.

• Google Cloud Vision Web Detection: Returns web entities and pages with matching
images. Useful as a lead generator for where an image appears online. Not a face recognition
system on its own.
• PimEyes: Powerful consumer face search tool, but no confirmed public developer API for
SaaS embedding was found. Key gating factors for Whair would be contractual embedding
rights, biometric data processing terms, and jurisdictional compliance. Requires explicit
vendor agreement.
• Bing Visual Search: Risk flag. Microsoft’s Bing Search APIs were reported decommissioned
for many developers as of August 2025. Do not build core logic around this.

Recommended Detection Approach for Beta
Start with a combination of AWS Rekognition for face matching (user-submitted URLs and uploaded
media) and TinEye for web occurrence discovery. Use Cloud Vision Web Detection as an optional
secondary signal. Build your own face embedding pipeline using open-source models (ArcFace
family) with Weaviate as the vector store, which is exactly the architecture Loti validated at scale.
This lets us start with vendor APIs for speed while building toward our own infrastructure.

What Feels Obtainable
The beta goal structure you laid out is the right one. Verification as the excellent pillar, Detection and
Enforcement as demo-ready, Monetization as conceptual. Here is why this is obtainable and how I
would approach the 30-day sprint:

Verification (Excellent)
This is our fastest, cheapest, and most differentiated build. The C2PA SDK is open source and
supports images and video. We can ship a creator enrollment flow (canonical assets tied to identity
with signed registration records), a verified content export feature (creators generate C2PA-signed
images and video for official releases), and a provenance viewer (upload any asset and see whether
it has provenance metadata or not). YouTube’s adoption of C2PA-based labels gives us a real
ecosystem adoption story to tell anchors. None of our competitors are doing this.

Detection (Demo-Ready)
Scoped as targeted discovery rather than internet-scale crawling. Creator enrolls with a face
reference set (5-20 images). Users can submit suspected misuse URLs for face matching, and we
run scheduled monitoring on a small set of creator-provided handles and pages. Incidents are stored
with URLs, screenshots, match scores, timestamps, and hashes for evidence packaging. AWS
Rekognition handles face detection and comparison within the free tier at beta volume. TinEye
handles web occurrence lookups at $200/month for 5,000 searches.

Enforcement (Demo-Ready)
Semi-manual with a clear workflow. Each incident gets an action center: generate a takedown packet
(URLs, screenshots, timestamps, hashes, statement of rights, provenance evidence), route to the
correct platform reporting form with pre-filled information where possible, and track status (submitted,
acknowledged, removed, denied). We integrate with YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, and X reporting
entry points. No major API costs. Enterprise takedown vendors like Red Points and BrandShield are
all quote-based enterprise sales, so we build our own workflow engine for now and consider
partnerships later once we validate demand.

Monetization (Conceptual)
Present in the UI as a licensing decision flow. When a detection is found, the creator can choose to
authorize it with a price or request removal. If authorized, a basic Stripe Connect payment flow
handles the transaction. This does not need to be a full marketplace at beta, it just needs to show
that the loop closes: detect, verify, enforce or monetize. Stripe Connect is pay-as-you-go with
negligible costs at beta volume.

Things to Flag

A few things worth discussing before we start building:
• Biometric data handling. We are storing face images and potentially voice samples. This
has real privacy and security implications even at beta. We need to be intentional about
encryption, storage architecture (store embeddings not raw media wherever possible), and
deletion semantics.
• Metadata stripping. Platforms and screenshots can strip C2PA metadata. This is a known
ecosystem issue. Our verification story needs to account for the fact that provenance data
does not always survive distribution. The provenance viewer inside Whair becomes the
verification point of record.
• Voice scope for beta. I recommend scoping voice as a registration and verification feature at
beta (creator registers voiceprint as proof of ownership) and phasing in active voice scanning
later as the tooling matures. This is an honest position and no competitor has solved it either.
• UI alignment. Before building, we should align on the exact feature set for each pillar so the
UI reflects what is actually shipping in the beta versus what comes later.
